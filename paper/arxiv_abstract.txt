Generative world models and 3D Gaussian Splatting promise to turn ordinary video into explorable 3D scenes, but little is reported on how they behave on uncurated, in-the-wild footage. We study what these models can and cannot recover when the only input is a publicly available news walkthrough of a food bank warehouse, with no drawings, scans or site visit, and we use the result as the geometry of a digital twin. A commercial world model given 20 frames recovered camera poses and metric depth for three areas, which we fused into about 747,000 Gaussian splats for 200 credits (20 US cents). Splat models optimized locally from 49 to 72 frames per area, at no credit cost, were photorealistic near the filmed path but degraded quickly away from it. Asking the world model to generate views of areas the video never showed failed on the provider's side twice, so unseen space was filled by a scripted Blender model instead. We describe the coordinate and packaging corrections needed to turn world-model output into a valid, metric Universal Scene Description (OpenUSD) asset using its native splat schema, and use that format as the only interface to a serverless data layer, a browser viewer, Blender and NVIDIA Omniverse. Omniverse rendered the modeled facility with RTX but drew the splat prims as blank grey. Because a sensor-fed twin brings operational technology (OT) into scope, we also give a zone-based OT security design in which the twin can read the floor but never write to it; only its read-only cloud controls are implemented. The findings suggest that today's models recover the observed parts of a real scene cheaply and at metric scale, while extrapolating unobserved space, the step a whole-building twin needs most, remains the open problem. The work is an exploration of these methods, not a deployed product.
