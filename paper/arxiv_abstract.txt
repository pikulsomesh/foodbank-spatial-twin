Generative world models and 3D Gaussian Splatting promise to turn ordinary
video into explorable 3D scenes, but little is reported on how they behave on
uncurated, in-the-wild footage. We study what these models recover when the
only input is a publicly available news walkthrough of a food bank warehouse,
with presenters walking through every shot and no drawings, scans or site
visit, and we use the result as the geometry of a digital twin. A commercial
world model given 20 frames recovered camera poses and metric depth for three
areas, which we fused into about 747,000 Gaussian splats for 200 credits (20
US cents). Splat models optimized locally on a CPU, at no credit cost, were
photorealistic near the filmed path but degraded quickly away from it. On
held-out frames, scored in pixels not covered by people, they reached 21.2 and
23.1 dB PSNR from 64 and 43 training frames, and lost 3.8 and 5.4 dB when
given only the 8 frames a world model accepts. Removing the trainer's background fill and person-hull
carving raised held-out PSNR, showing that cleaning the space behind moving
people costs fidelity in observed pixels while its gain lies where no ground
truth exists. Asking the world model to generate views of areas the video
never showed failed on the provider's side twice, so extrapolation could not
be evaluated and unseen space was filled by a scripted model instead. We also
describe the corrections needed to turn world-model output into a valid,
metric Universal Scene Description (OpenUSD) asset with its native splat
schema, which serves as the only
interface to a data layer, a browser viewer, Blender and NVIDIA Omniverse. The
findings suggest that today's models recover the observed parts of a real
scene cheaply and at metric scale, while few-view fidelity and extrapolating
unobserved space remain open. The work is an exploration of these methods,
not a deployed product.
